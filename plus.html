Lesson 1

Left-hand practice

Your fretting hand does most of the learning in the first months. This lesson starts with reading tab, the simplest way guitar music is written, then puts it to work with short melodies that train each finger to land cleanly on its fret.

1.1

Reading tabs

Tab (short for tablature) is the simplest way guitar music is written down. It doesn't show notes or rhythm. It shows where to put your fingers: which string, which fret. Most songs, riffs and solos you'll find online are written in tab.

  • Six lines, six stringsThe top line is the thinnest string (high e), the bottom line the thickest (low E).
  • Numbers are fretsA 3 on a line means: press that string at the 3rd fret and pick it.
  • 0 means openPick the string without pressing anything.
  • Read left to rightPlay the numbers in order. Numbers stacked on top of each other are played together, as a chord.

Heads-up: tab is drawn upside down compared with what you see when you look down at your guitar. Looking down, the thick low E is closest to you, at the top. In tab, it's the bottom line. It feels odd for a day or two, then it clicks.

1. The open strings

Tap a number to hear it

All zeros: every string played open, from the thickest to the thinnest. Notice how the 0 climbs from the bottom line to the top.

2. A first melody

“Ode to Joy” by Beethoven, on just the top two strings. You only need two fingers: index on fret 1, ring finger on fret 3. Tap any number, or press Play and follow the highlight.

3. Chords in tab

Tap a column to strum it

Stacked numbers are a chord. Here are G, C and D from the free guide. Compare each column with the chord diagram you already know: same frets, just written sideways. A string with no number isn't played.

1.2

Practice task

Three short, well-known melodies, played from tab. They start on the top two strings and then bring in the G string.

One finger per fret

These melodies train your fretting hand to move cleanly between frets. Give each fret its own finger, the same numbers as on the chord cards:

  • 0open, no finger
  • 1fret 1 · index
  • 2fret 2 · middle
  • 3fret 3 · ring
  • 4fret 4–5 · pinky
  1. Start slow, at 60 BPM. Play along and follow the highlight.
  2. Press with your fingertip, just behind the fret.
  3. Keep the other fingers hovering close to the strings, ready to drop.
  4. Lift a finger straight up instead of sliding it.
  5. When a melody sounds clean three times in a row, add 10 BPM.
60 BPM

Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star

A traditional tune. The A note sits at the 5th fret, so stretch your pinky up to it and keep your index finger near fret 1.

Jingle Bells

The chorus, from 1857. Lots of repeated notes: keep your fingers relaxed and let the open e string ring between fretted notes.

Mary Had a Little Lamb

A traditional tune that adds the 3rd string, G. Your hand moves across strings now: middle finger on the G string at fret 2, ring finger on the B string at fret 3.

Lesson 2

The neck and its notes

Every fret is a note with a name. You don't need to memorize the whole neck. Knowing how the names work, and the notes on the two lowest strings, gets you a long way: it's how you'll find barre chords later.

The musical alphabet

Music uses only seven letters: A B C D E F G, then it starts again at A. Between most letters sits one extra note, called a sharp (♯) or a flat (♭). A♯ and B♭ are the same note with two names.

AA♯ B♭BCC♯ D♭DD♯ E♭EFF♯ G♭GG♯ A♭

Twelve notes in all. Note that B–C and E–F have nothing in between.

  • One fret = one stepMoving up one fret moves one box along the alphabet above. Open A string, 1st fret A♯, 2nd fret B, 3rd fret C.
  • The 12th fret starts overTwelve frets up, you're back at the same note as the open string, just higher. That's why the 12th fret has a double dot.

Explore the neck

Tap any spot to hear it and see its name. Start with the bottom two strings, low E and A, outlined in orange.

Find the note

A quick game on the low E and A strings. Hide the names first if you want a real challenge.
